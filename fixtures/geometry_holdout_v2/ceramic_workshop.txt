The workshop opened before the visitors arrived. Lina placed six unfinished bowls on the long table and covered the remaining clay with a damp cloth. The bowls looked similar from the doorway, but each had a different problem. One leaned slightly to the left, one had a heavy base, and another carried a fine crack just below the rim. She wanted the morning class to examine these differences before touching the wheels.

When the students gathered, Lina asked them to describe what they could observe without proposing a repair. A polished surface did not necessarily mean that a wall had an even thickness. A circular opening could sit above an uneven body. Several students reached immediately for their tools, then laughed and put them down. The exercise was slower than they expected. It required turning each bowl, looking at its shadow, and comparing the inside with the outside.

Lina gave each pair a small notebook. They drew the outline of their bowl and marked the places where the clay felt unusually thick. The marks were observations, she explained, rather than instructions to remove material. The clay near the foot still had to support the wall. Cutting away too much could make a pleasing shape collapse during the next stage. They would make one modest change, examine the result, and decide whether another change was justified.

At the far end of the table, Omar discovered that his bowl seemed uneven because the board beneath it was warped. He exchanged the board before trying to reshape the clay. His partner had already noticed a second problem: the rim was drying faster than the lower wall. They covered the rim loosely while they discussed their options. Neither action made the bowl finished, but both made the next observation more useful.

After lunch, the class compared its first drawings with the altered bowls. Some repairs were almost invisible. Others had improved one side while making the opposite side less balanced. Lina kept these examples together instead of choosing only the most successful pieces for display. The unfinished work would become the starting point for the following lesson, when the students could explain not only what they had changed, but also why they had stopped.
